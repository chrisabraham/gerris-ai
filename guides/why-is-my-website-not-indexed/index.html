Why is my website not indexed by Google?

A page that is not in Google's index does not exist for search. It cannot rank, it cannot appear in AI Overviews, and the answer engines that draw on search indexes cannot cite it. Indexing is the floor under every other kind of visibility, and it is the first thing Gerris checks on any site, because the most common reason a business is invisible is also the plainest one: the pages were never admitted to the index in the first place. This guide covers why pages get left out, how to check yours, and the order of fixes that gets them in. The repair work itself is on the services page.

What does it mean for a page to be indexed?

Google's system runs in three stages. A crawler fetches the page. The index stores what the crawler found, organized so it can be retrieved. Serving answers queries from the index, not from the live web. A page can clear the first stage and fail the second: Google crawled it, read it, and decided to leave it out. Search Console reports this state in so many words, "Crawled - currently not indexed," and it is a decision, not an error. The page was weighed as a candidate for the index and the index declined it, usually because the same content exists elsewhere in a stronger form, or because the page gives the index too little that is new to store.

The second failure state is "Discovered - currently not indexed." Google knows the URL exists, from a sitemap or a link, and has not yet spent a crawl on it. On a small site this clears on its own. On a site that publishes faster than it earns attention, the discovered queue grows, and the pages at the back of it can wait months.

What blocks a page from being indexed at all?

Some pages never reach the decision stage because an instruction stops them first. The blocks, in the order the desk finds them:

  • A noindex instruction: a meta tag or an X-Robots-Tag header telling every engine to leave the page out. Development sites ship with it on; migrations carry it live by accident.
  • A robots.txt block: the crawler is forbidden to fetch the page, so Google can only list a bare URL with no content, or drop it entirely.
  • A canonical tag pointing elsewhere: the page declares another URL the real version of itself, and the index keeps the other one.
  • Server and redirect failures: the page returns an error, a redirect chain that dies, or a soft 404, a page that says "not found" in its text while returning a success code.
  • Orphan pages: no internal link leads to the page, so the crawler has no path to it and the index has no signal that the site itself values it.
  • Login walls and rendering failures: the content sits behind a sign-in, or it only exists after scripts run that the crawler could not complete.

Every item on that list is mechanical, and every one is fixable. Blocks like these are why Gerris treats indexing as a repair trade before it treats it as a strategy question.

Why does Google index a page and then drop it?

Because the index is a store with standards, not an archive of everything crawled. Pages fall out when the store re-weighs them: the content thinned after an edit, a stronger page on the same site started answering the same query, the page's links disappeared in a redesign, or the site as a whole lost the crawl attention that kept marginal pages in. Duplicate content is the steady cause. When three pages say the same thing in nearly the same words, the index keeps the one it judges the original and releases the other two, and the released pages are usually the ones the business expected to rank.

How do you check whether your pages are indexed?

Four checks, in order. First, the site search: type site:yourdomain.com into Google and compare the count against the number of pages you publish. It is rough and it is fast. Second, the Page indexing report in Search Console, which lists every URL Google knows about on your site and the exact reason each excluded page was excluded. Third, the URL Inspection tool on your money pages one at a time: it reports the page's index state, its canonical as Google sees it, and when it was last crawled. Fourth, your sitemap: a sitemap that lists URLs the index has dropped, or omits the pages you care about, is telling you where the disagreement lives. The desk runs these four before it recommends anything, because the fix for a blocked page and the fix for a declined page are different jobs.

How do you get a page indexed?

Remove the block first, if there is one: lift the noindex, open the robots.txt rule, repair the canonical, fix the error. Then give the crawler a path and a reason. Link the page from indexed pages that matter, in the body text, with anchor text that says what the page is. List it in the sitemap. Make the page worth storing: a clear statement of its subject in the words buyers search, enough substance that the index gains something by keeping it, and no near-twin elsewhere on the site competing for the same slot. Request indexing in Search Console once the page is fixed, as a notification, not a lever. Then wait and verify in the same reports you checked at the start. Indexing earned this way holds. Indexing begged for, on a page that is still blocked or still a duplicate, does not.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take Google to index a new page?

On an established site with internal links pointing at it, a new page is usually crawled within days and indexed within a week or two. On a new site, or a page with no links and no sitemap entry, the same page can wait months. The difference is rarely the page. It is the crawl attention the site has earned and the paths the site gives the crawler.

Does requesting indexing in Search Console guarantee a page gets indexed?

No. Request indexing asks for a crawl. It does not overrule a noindex tag, a robots.txt block, a canonical pointing elsewhere, or Google's judgment that the page duplicates a stronger one. Fix the cause first, then request. The request is the last step of a repair, not a substitute for one.

Why was my page dropped from the index?

The usual causes are duplication, thinning, and neglect: another page began answering the same query better, an edit removed the substance the index was storing, or a redesign removed the internal links that kept the page in circulation. Search Console's Page indexing report names the state Google put the page in, and that state is where the repair starts.
