How do you turn a manufacturer's feed into premium pages?

Every online store that sells somebody else's brand starts from the same file. The manufacturer hands each reseller one database, each reseller pours it into a store, and within a week the web holds ten thousand copies of a catalog that was written once. Nothing in that file belongs to the store that published it. The premium a store can actually charge—over the identical product, offered by a hundred rivals off the identical data—is built afterward, product by product. That building is the subject of this guide.

What actually arrives in a manufacturer feed?

A part number and not much else. The identifiers come first: the SKU, the maker's own code for the item, and often an ASIN, Amazon's identifier, riding along beside it. Around them sit a price, a table of specifications, a short description composed by somebody at the factory, and a handful of photographs—usually the same handful, shot against the same white background, that every dealer receives. Kawasaki sends this file. Seiko sends this file. Every brand that reaches customers through other people's storefronts sends some version of it, because the file is how the product exists at wholesale. The photographs deserve a word of their own: since every dealer receives the same shots, the images carry no mark of any particular store, and a reverse image search on a product photo returns a directory of everyone selling that product. What the file never contains is a reason to buy the item from you.

Why does an imported page have to win two fights at once?

Publish the feed as it lands and your product page enters two contests it did not train for. The first is global. Every other store that imported the same database published the same title, the same description, the same photographs, at roughly the same price. A search engine comparing those pages has almost nothing to choose between except price and shipping speed, and a comparison shopper has even less. The second contest runs inside your own store. Ten thousand products described in one factory voice, at one length, in one rhythm, blur together; the diver's watch and the dress watch end up sounding like a single watch listed twice. A page indistinguishable from its rivals abroad is indistinguishable from its neighbors at home as well. Diversity is not decoration here. It is the only axis left once the product itself is identical everywhere.

What gets finished on every product page?

The work is per item, and it is specific. Each product gets image alt text written for its own photographs—what the picture actually shows, the angle, the detail a buyer is meant to notice—not the file name the feed supplied. Each product gets a title built to stand alone on a results page, inside sixty characters, saying which item this is and what sets it apart. Each gets a snippet description of a hundred and sixty characters, composed for that item instead of trimmed down from the factory paragraph. Each gets schema of its own: structured data naming this product, this offer, this store's terms, rather than a template stamped across the whole catalog. And each gets its description—the real one, the body of the page—rewritten from the ground up. That last job has a name in my practice. I call it a J. Peterman premium snow job, and it deserves a section of its own.

What is a J. Peterman premium snow job?

The J. Peterman catalog sold ordinary goods—shirts, bags, coats—at extraordinary prices for decades, and it did it with paragraphs. A Peterman description opens somewhere: a place, a weather, a person caught mid-gesture. It gets specific in the senses—the weight of a cloth, the smell of a leather, the work a thing was made to perform. It tells you who you are while you own the item: the traveler, the expatriate, the one who knows. Then, just as the romance peaks, it drops into blunt inventory. Real horn buttons. Two deep pockets. Imported. The specifications land harder for having been made to wait.

I know the technique works because it worked on me. In the eighties I wanted a French sailor's shirt with blue stripes because of how it was described, and I wanted a leather postal bag priced in the hundreds, and I still half want that bag today. Nothing about either object changed. The story laid on top of it did. That is the whole mechanism: a commodity carrying a story nobody else is telling steps out of the comparison table. Rivals can match a price overnight. They cannot adopt your narrative without visibly copying it, so the item stops competing on the one axis where the feed made everybody equal.

Who is the copy for, besides the buyer who already knows the part number?

One catalog I work on sells fittings—industrial parts, threads, tolerances. Its established customers are mechanical engineers, and they arrive knowing everything: the part number, the product name, the exact thread the job requires. Copy written for them alone is a lookup table, and a lookup table is all that catalog would ever become. The buyers it never reaches are the ones holding a problem instead of a part number. They search by what a fitting is used for, what it was designed to do, how somebody adapted it to a job it was not designed for, what it held together in an earlier installation and at what tolerance. None of that language lives in a manufacturer feed, because the feed was written for the warehouse. Writing it in—uses, cases, worked examples, the imagination of the thing—is the other half of the snow job. The engineer keeps his specifications, stated plainly where he expects them. The solution searcher finally gets a page that answers in his vocabulary.

Where does the snow job cross the line?

Story has a failure mode, and it loops. Take milk that was never chemically treated and never genetically modified in the first place. Advertise it for everything it is not: not GMO. No lead. No radium. Not contaminated with uranium. Every claim on the carton is true, and the carton gets stranger with each one, until a shopper arrives at the paradox: why is this man talking about radiation in his milk? What is he trying to hide? Premium language pushed past that point stops reading as abundance and starts reading as concealment. The discipline is to add the story a product can honestly carry—its making, its uses, its history in people's hands—and to stop before the claims turn into a list of denials. A snow job sells what a thing is, dressed in its best light. The moment it starts selling what the thing is not, the circle closes and the premium curdles into suspicion. It is also wasteful: each invented denial spends the shopper's attention, which was the entire budget the story was written to earn.

What am I actually selling?

Earlier in my career, at Walmart and at GE through Edelman, the assignment was always some version of this one: take a generic, commoditized product and find the premium inside it, the version that could carry a premium price. I have done that work ever since, except that the shelf moved. I am not a product salesman. I am a page salesman. I sell pages, as SEO, and my pages have to become premium pages—pages that surface when somebody searches, and that hold their own against the plain-Jane API import every other dropshipper received from the same manufacturer. The same finishing hand that separates one page of a website from another separates one product from another here, item by item. What the feed gives everyone is the product. What a store adds, on top of it, is the only property that store will ever exclusively own.

What should you do next?

Count the exposure first. Take fifty products at random from your catalog and set your pages beside the same fifty in two rival stores; whatever matches word for word is feed, and feed is what the whole world already has. Frequently asked questions

Can a store just import the feed and leave it?

It can, and most do, which is precisely the problem. An untouched import publishes the manufacturer's words under your domain, identical to the same words under every rival's domain. Search engines fold identical pages toward a single representative, and shoppers fold them toward the lowest delivered price. Leaving the feed alone does not hold a store neutral; it enters the store in the one contest—price—it is least equipped to win.

Does a snow job mean inventing things about a product?

No. The romance is assembled from material that exists: where the design came from, what it was made to do, who uses it and for what, how it wears after a year of work. Invention is the failure the line warns about. A story a buyer can check and find true compounds; one he catches out poisons his trust in every other page of the catalog. Specifications stay exact and checkable, printed in the open, underneath the narrative.

Is this realistic across a hundred thousand SKUs?

In layers. The metadata layer—alt text, a title of its own, a snippet description, product schema—can and should reach every SKU, because agents make that pass affordable at catalog scale. The full narrative pass goes first to the products carrying the margin, the demand, or the competition to justify it, and spreads outward from there. A catalog does not need every page finished to the same depth. It needs no page left exactly as the feed delivered it.

What if my customers search by part number only?

Some do, and the page should honor that: the SKU, the name, the thread, the tolerances, stated early and exactly. But a page can hold both audiences at once. Put the identifiers where the engineer scans for them, and put the uses, the cases, and the worked examples below, where the buyer with a problem is reading. The first audience finds the part. The second audience finds your store.
