How AI engines read your business: literal words, mandatory summaries, consistent facts

By Chris Abraham, Gerris AI. Published Tuesday, November 17, 2026.

AI engines read your business literally, summarize it whether you take part or not, and name the businesses whose facts agree everywhere the engines look. Those are the three working rules behind every Gerris engagement, and they appear in short form in the field notes on the home page. This post states them at full length: what a literal reader does to your copy, why the summary of your business gets written with or without you, and how consistency across your website, your Google Business Profile, and your listings became the signal that decides whose version of the facts an answer repeats.

What does it mean that the engines read literally?

Google and the AI answer engines take your words at face value. If a phrase is missing from the page, in the form buyers actually ask it, the page does not come up for that phrase. There is no thesaurus at the other end, and nobody at Google is inferring what you meant. A search system matches the words in the question against words it can find in its sources, and the answer engines build on that same matching before they write a single sentence of their own.

This surprises owners who wrote their sites for human readers with patience. A human visitor forgives a home page that calls the firm a solutions provider while the words estate planning attorney sit three clicks deep; the human can guess, browse, and telephone. The engine works from the text it retrieved. Category language that lives in a printed brochure, a trade-show banner, or the owner's head never enters the record, and a record entry that never happened cannot be summarized, cited, or named.

The practical consequence is plain. The words buyers type and speak—emergency plumber near me, CPA for restaurants, who sells standing desks in Austin—have to appear on your pages in those forms, attached to your name, your place, and your prices where prices are public. Synonyms chosen for elegance are a private vocabulary. The engine keeps the buyer's words and discards yours wherever the two differ.

Why does the summary get written whether you take part or not?

Being summarized is mandatory. You do not get to opt out of being described by AI answers. You only choose whether the description is assembled from your facts or from your competitor's.

Every business with a website, a profile, or a single review already has an AI description. The engines assembled it from whatever they could find: the site, the Google Business Profile, directory listings, review pages, and anything third parties published. A firm that has never thought about AI search is participating at full strength; it simply never read its own entry. When a buyer asks who to hire, the answer reads that entry aloud, in the buyer's hearing, at the moment of decision.

This changes what doing nothing costs. Under the old results page, a business that ignored search could still be found by referral and foot traffic while it ignored the list, and the list said nothing about it beyond a title and a line of text. The answer layer speaks about the business either way, in the business's absence, from sources the business may never have read. Authorship of the record is the only choice on offer. Gerris's work starts from that fact: the description exists, so the business may as well write it.

How does an engine decide which version of your facts to repeat?

It goes with the version it can corroborate. An answer engine resolves many sources into one record of your business, and it trusts the version of each fact that the sources agree on. When your website, your Google Business Profile, and your listings state the same category, the same services, and the same phone number, that fact is settled and usable. When the sources disagree, the engine reports the version it could verify, and the version it could verify may be the stale one.

Consistency is a ranking signal in the plainest sense. One story, told identically everywhere, is what the machines reward. The reward is clerical: a fact that survives corroboration can be stated in an answer plainly, and answers are built from facts the engine can state. A fact tangled in contradiction costs the engine confidence, and the answer's names go to the businesses that cost it the least. The guide to how AI answers choose which businesses to name walks through that selection step by step.

What breaks when your website, your profile, and your listings disagree?

The failures are mundane, and the audits meet the same ones every month. The website calls the business a plumber, the profile calls it a handyman service, and an old listing calls it a general contractor: three blurred records where one clear one should stand. The address changed on the site and stayed old in the listings. A phone number from a previous office keeps circulating in the directories. The founding service, the one buyers ask about by name, appears on a printed brochure and nowhere the crawlers go.

Each conflict is small. Together they blur the record, and a blurred record earns a vague description, a description of a competitor, or silence. Read the skip list again and notice what it never contains: the quality of the business's work. The engine reports on paperwork, and paperwork can be fixed. Every item on the skip list has a clerical remedy, and the remedies are the same ones the guide to generative engine optimization sets out in pipeline order.

How should a business write for a literal reader?

The working checklist behind every Gerris engagement follows from the three rules:

  • State the category in so many words: one canonical page that says what the business is, what it sells, where it works, and what it charges where prices are public.
  • Write headings as the questions buyers ask: the answer goes directly under each heading, at the top of the section, in the buyer's language.
  • Repeat the record identically: the same name, address, phone number, category, and services on the website, the profile, and the listings, word for word where the formats allow.
  • Publish facts in liftable form: direct answers, honest FAQ content, and published prices give the engine sentences it can quote without rewriting them.
  • Hand the machines the whole record at once: entity schema with sameAs links and an llms.txt file tell every crawler and language model where the canonical statement lives.

None of this is decoration, and none of it asks an engine for a favor. The engine is under pressure to answer quickly and defensibly, and it reaches for the sources that cost it the least effort and the least risk. A business whose facts arrive pre-assembled, consistently, in quotable sentences is simply the easiest correct answer available.

How do you check what the machines say about you today?

You ask, in your buyers' words, and you write down the answers. Put your category and your city to Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini. Record whether you are named, whether the description matches your facts, and who is named in your place. Then compare the answers against your website, your profile, and your listings, and find where the record disagrees with itself. That comparison is the diagnosis, and the fixes follow it in priority order.

Frequently asked questions

Do AI engines understand synonyms and implied meaning?

They work from the words on the page. A search system matches the words in the buyer's question against words it can find in its sources, and the answer engines build on that matching. A category stated only in elegant synonyms, or implied by photographs and reputation, is a category the record may never receive. State what the business is in the words buyers use to ask for it.

Can a business stop AI answers from describing it?

The description is assembled from public sources: the website, the Google Business Profile, directory listings, reviews, and pages other people publish. A business can withdraw from some of those sources and shrink what the engines can find, and the answer will then describe it from whatever remains, or name a competitor whose record is complete. The working choice is authorship: write the record yourself, in your own facts, and keep it consistent.

What is the first consistency fix to make?

Settle one canonical statement of the business: the name, the category, the services, the address or service area, and the phone number. Then bring the Google Business Profile and the highest-traffic directory listings into line with that statement, word for word where the formats allow. Most records improve measurably from that single pass, because corroboration starts working for the business instead of against it.

How often do AI answers about a business change?

Continuously, on the engines' own schedule. Competitors publish, directories change, profiles get edited, and the answer that named a business in March may name another in June. A single check is a snapshot. Gerris measures monthly, re-asking the same money questions across Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini, because the trend across months is the measurement that can be acted on.
